# The map of `src/ui/`

What every file of the interface holds.
Every sentence is on a line of its own: Grep the map for a file or a word, and read it whole only
to plan a change across the layer. It is not loaded with `CLAUDE.md`, which keeps the rules.

- `src/ui/`: Vue components in `ui/components/`, composables (`use*.ts`) and plain helpers (the other `.ts` files).
  `App.vue` keeps one CPU per architecture (`useCpu(X86)`, `useCpu(RV32)`, `useCpu(MOS6502)`) and shows the workbench of the one chosen in the header (`X86Workbench.vue`, `Rv32Workbench.vue`, `Mos6502Workbench.vue`), each loaded when first shown (the others are fetched once the page is idle) and kept alive after that, so switching back is instant.
  A workbench lays out its panels in the shared columns and gives `useWorkbench.ts` its hooks (which programs use the devices, what a step sounds like, the page actions, which `useWorkbenchPage.ts` builds from its `builder` and `memoryView` refs); `useProgramCode.ts` holds its program as text and machine code and what the machine code panel shows; `AppChrome.vue` is the header, palette and dialogs around the panels.
  Shared panels (`ProgramPanel`, `HistoryLog`, `DevicesPanel`, `InstructionBuilder`) take an architecture's differences as props: a format function, `LineCode` (`programCode.ts`), `DeviceAccess` (`deviceAccess.ts`), and an `InstructionGrammar` (`builderGrammar.ts`; `x86Grammar.ts`, `rv32Grammar.ts`, `mos6502Grammar.ts`).
  The builder's choices are worked out in `useBuilderOptions.ts` and its arrow keys in `useBuilderArrows.ts`; `BuilderTokenBox.vue`, `BuilderOptions.vue` and `BuilderActions.vue` draw its word box, its options and its buttons.
  A program line is edited in the builder: a grammar's `parse` turns the line back into words, and `useProgramLines.ts` holds which line is being edited, and keeps the lines locked at the top of the program (a challenge's given lines, counted by `keptGiven` and passed in by `useCpu`) from being edited, moved, removed or pushed down, by the rules of `programLock.ts`; `ProgramLineActions.vue` is a line's buttons and menu.
  A CPU's program is saved to localStorage and read back through `programStorage.ts`, which drops anything the architecture does not accept.
  `FlatMemory.vue` (with `flatMemory.ts`) is the memory panel of the flat address spaces, RISC-V's and the 6502's; the 8086's `MemoryView.vue` works out its rows and addresses in `x86MemoryView.ts`, and both colour a recent write with `memoryHeat.ts`.
  Panels of one architecture are named for it (`Rv32Diagram.vue`, `Mos6502Diagram.vue`, …; the 8086's kept their original names), and their view logic is in plain helpers: RISC-V's in `rv32Explain.ts`, `rv32Datapath.ts`, `rv32View.ts` and `rv32Pipeline.ts` (the pipeline chart, shown by `Rv32Pipeline.vue` in place of the cycle strip, with its switches, forwarding and the branch predictor, drawn by `Rv32PipelineOptions.vue` and held in `useRv32Pipeline.ts`; `useRv32Timing.ts` works the timing out once, a step at a time, for the chart and for the sound of a step alike), the 6502's in `mos6502Explain.ts`, `mos6502Alu.ts` (the ALU's sum and why each flag is set), `mos6502Diagram.ts` and `mos6502View.ts`.
  The 8086's cycle text is in `x86Explain.ts`, with the condition of each conditional jump in `x86Jumps.ts`; `explainText.ts` holds the words the three explanations share (why a jump was taken or not).
  `hexNotation.ts` is how each CPU writes hex (`2Ah`, `0x2A`, `$2A`), and `useHexStyle.ts` hands a topic's page the one of the CPU chosen in the header.
  `useCpu.ts` wraps an architecture in Vue reactivity, built from `useCpuError.ts` (the error on show), `useCpuHistory.ts` (undo, redo, seek), `useProgramLines.ts` (editing the program), `useProgramRun.ts` (running on a timer) and `usePhaseStepping.ts` (fetch, decode, execute).
  `useCpuDiagram.ts` works out what the CPU diagram shows, and its parts (`Diagram*.vue`) only draw it.
  `CpuCanvas.vue` is the CPU panel the three diagrams share: its header, and the flat sheet the chip is drawn on.
  The sidebar is `SidebarColumn.vue`: the reorderable `SortableColumn` (a panel is dragged by its header, or moved with Alt and the arrows on its `data-sortable-handle` button; `useSortableDrag.ts` and `useSortableKeys.ts`) with each panel wrapped in a `SidebarSection.vue` (collapse, close, and the line that resizes it); the stage is a fixed `StageColumn.vue`, its state in `useSidebarLayout.ts` (what is collapsed and each panel's size, remembered) and `useSidebarResize.ts`, its rules in `sidebarLayout.ts`.
  A panel marks the list that should take its room with `data-fill`.
  The layout follows the window's width (`layoutMode.ts`, read through `useLayoutMode.ts`; the style sheets switch at the same 48rem and 64rem): two panes on a desktop, the sidebar as a drawer over the stage on a tablet, and on a phone one of three views (`ViewTabs.vue`: Code, CPU, Memory & I/O).
  `useWorkbenchView.ts` holds which view or drawer is on show, `PaneFrame.vue` is the two panes of any workbench (with `ViewTabs.vue`, a phone's views of them, and the drawer every page shares), `WorkbenchPanes.vue` is the CPU's three views of them, and `stagePanels.ts` lists the stage's panels and the phone view of each; hidden panels stay mounted.
  Below a desktop `ResultStrip.vue` (filled by each architecture's `*ChangeStrip.vue`) keeps the last result in sight.
  `TransportBar.vue` is put together from `TransportRun`, `TransportSpeed`, `TransportHistory` and `TransportPanels`, with `TransportMore` holding what a narrow bar has no room for.
  The run buttons take their words from `transportLabels.ts`, and `DisabledReason.vue` lets a button that is off say why.
  `useKeyboardInset.ts` follows a touch screen's on-screen keyboard, and `TapTooltip.vue` is a tooltip a tap opens too.
  `useCpuReset.ts` decides when a reset asks first, and runs a finished program again.
  The stack has a panel of its own on the stage, `StackPanel.vue`, which draws a `StackView` (`stackView.ts`: a row per value from the top down, what it is, the instruction that put it there, and what the last instruction pushed or took off).
  Each architecture reads its own (`x86Stack.ts`: words from SS:SP; `mos6502Stack.ts`: the bytes of page $01, a pushed address as one row; `rv32Stack.ts`: the words from sp to the top of RAM, since no instruction pushes), `stackWriters.ts` keeps which record wrote each byte last, following a history that grows without reading it again, and `useStack.ts` works the view out only while the panel reads it.
  Whether it is shown is `useStackShown` (`usePanels.ts`), flipped from the transport bar and the palette.
  Prediction mode asks what an instruction will do before it runs: `prediction.ts` holds a `Question` (whether a branch is taken, the value a register will hold, or the flags of a comparison), the `Answer`, how one is graded and a `Predictor`, an architecture's side of it (`x86Prediction.ts`, `rv32Prediction.ts`, `mos6502Prediction.ts`: the question an instruction deserves, the truth read from its record and the CPU right after it, never from a trial run, and the existing explanation as the reason).
  `usePrediction.ts` holds the question waiting (the last answer and the run's tally are `usePredictionScore.ts`'s), and gives a workbench its `controls`: the CPU with Step, the last phase, the builder's Run and Run (`usePredictionRun.ts`, a run that waits at each question) going through the question, which is what a workbench hands `useWorkbench` and its transport bar in place of the CPU.
  `PredictionBar.vue` (with `PredictionAnswer.vue`) is the question and the verdict above the transport bar, with the focus around a question in `usePredictionFocus.ts`; the mode and where it asks are settings of `usePreferences.ts`.
  `useCommands.ts` builds the Ctrl+K command palette; a workbench adds the commands of its own panels through the `commands` hook of `useWorkbench`.
  `programLineCommands.ts` makes the palette's commands on the program line that runs next (edit, delete, move up or down, add new lines above it) and on where new lines go (back to the end), from the program as `ProgramLines` gives it, naming the lock of `programLock.ts` on a command a challenge forbids.
  The challenges: `useChallenges.ts` holds whether their list is open, the challenge each CPU is working on and the ones solved (the last two remembered, and read back through `storedRecord.ts`, which trusts nothing in storage), and `ChallengesDialog.vue` is the list, loaded the first time it opens; starting one (`startChallenge` in `useChallenge.ts`, wired in `App.vue`) loads its given lines as an example is loaded.
  `useChallenge.ts` judges a CPU's program against its challenge whenever the program changes, never on the CPU on show, and leaves the challenge when another program is loaded whole; `ChallengePanel.vue` is the sidebar's `challenge` panel (on show only while there is a challenge: closing it leaves it), with its text in `challengeView.ts`.
  `CompareDialog.vue` sets the comparison tasks side by side (its rows and text in `comparisonView.ts`); `useComparison.ts` holds whether it is open, so the header and the palette can open it, and it is loaded the first time it opens.
  Loading a task's program into a CPU (`loadProgram` in `useCpu`) is wired in `App.vue`, which holds the three CPUs.
  A program is shared as a link: `shareLink.ts` is the link's format (`share=<version>.<architecture>.<payload>` in the address's fragment: the stored program, deflated, in base64url) and reads one back through `parseProgram`, so a link is trusted no more than storage is; `useShare.ts` holds whether `ShareDialog.vue` is open (the header and the palette open it; it is loaded the first time, through `useLazyOpen.ts`, the open state the lazily loaded dialogs share), and `useShareLink.ts` makes the link of the program on show and copies it.
  `useSharedProgram.ts` reads a link from the address at startup and on `hashchange`, takes it out of the address, and opens its program at once or, when that would replace another program, after `SharedProgramDialog.vue` asked; the loading itself (`loadShared`) is wired in `App.vue`.
  `FeedbackDialog.vue` is the form to the developer, open from the activity bar, the header's menu below a desktop and the palette: `useFeedback.ts` holds whether it is open, its draft and the sending (a `POST` to `VITE_FEEDBACK_ENDPOINT`; without one a production build offers no feedback and development prints it), and `feedback.ts` what makes a draft sendable and the fields that are sent.
  `__APP_VERSION__` (a `define` in `vite.config.ts`, declared in `src/env.d.ts`) is the version sent with it.
  Usage is counted through three files, as sound is played: `analyticsEvents.ts` (the closed list of events; none carries what the user wrote), `analyticsClient.ts` (`analytics.track(event)`: loads Umami's script only when enabled, in a production build that names a site, and holds events until it has loaded) and the two composables that watch for them, `useCpuAnalytics.ts` (a CPU's first step, runs and errors, wired in `useWorkbench`) and `useAppAnalytics.ts` (the topic and CPU on show, wired once in `App.vue`); the few discrete actions (an example loaded, the gates, the numbers, a register or the control unit opened, a guess answered, feedback sent) are tracked where they happen.
  `usePreferences.ts` turns it on and off with the Share anonymous usage setting.
  The settings are one dialog, `SettingsDialog.vue`, open from the activity bar, the header's menu below a desktop and the palette (`useSettings.ts` holds whether it is open and its section; it is loaded the first time): the sections are listed in `settingsSections.ts` and each is a component of rows (`SettingsGeneral`, `SettingsAppearance`, `SettingsCpu`, `SettingsRun`, `SettingsKeyboard`, `SettingsData`, built from `SettingsRow`, `SettingsSwitch` and `SettingsChoice`).
  Every setting is a remembered ref of `usePreferences.ts` (the theme as light, dark or `auto`; the run speed, one for every CPU; whether the devices open by themselves; whether a reset asks first; prediction mode and where it asks) or of the panel's own composable (`useRv32Pipeline.ts`), so a panel's switch and the dialog's are one value.
  `shortcutList.ts` is the list the Keyboard section shows, and `storedData.ts` what Clear everything removes: every key of `storageKeys.ts` and each architecture's program.
  A new remembered setting gets a row there, and a key in `storageKeys.ts`.
  The topics: `topics.ts` lists them and `useTopic.ts` holds the one on show; `App.vue` shows `LogicWorkbench.vue`, `NumbersWorkbench.vue`, `StorageWorkbench.vue` or `ControlWorkbench.vue` in place of the CPU's workbench, and hands it the chosen CPU's `CpuMoment` (`cpuMoment.ts`: the last instruction, its link into that topic, and the nearest instructions the topic has something to show for), which `useCpuMoment.ts` works out from the three CPUs, once for each topic's linkers.
  The header is `HeaderShell.vue` (the bar, its mark, the commands) with a topic's own parts in its slots: `AppHeader.vue` fills them for the CPU.
  On a desktop the topics, feedback and the settings are the activity bar's, `ActivityBar.vue`: a narrow column down the left edge that `PaneFrame.vue` sets beside the two panes, between the header and the bar under the page, with the icon of each topic from `TopicIcon.vue` (which the ways between topics share).
  Below a desktop there is no activity bar and the header is compact: the commands are an icon, and the topics, a topic's buttons, feedback and the settings are rows of one menu; a button added to the header's `actions` needs its row in `menu-actions`.
  The link is live: a topic's page always shows the last record of the CPU on show, so stepping from it seeks that CPU, and going back lands on the instruction on show.
  Every topic's page is laid out in the shell of the CPU's workbench, `TopicWorkbench.vue`: the header, a sidebar of what is set, a stage of what is drawn (a container, so a section asks the stage's width, never the window's), and `TopicBar.vue` under both, in the place of the transport bar (the CPU's steps and Reset, the topic's own controls in its slot, and the ways on into the other topics, listed in `topicWays.ts`).
  The rule for what goes where: what is set once (the operation, the width, which circuit) is in the sidebar, and what is pressed again and again while watching (a bit, a clock) stays beside the drawing.
  The sidebar's sections are a topic's list of `TopicSectionInfo` (`topicSections.ts`), each a `TopicSection.vue` (a `SidebarSection` that folds but keeps its place, remembered by `useTopicSections.ts`) filled through the slot of its id, after the first, `TopicSource.vue` (where what is on show came from); unlike the CPU's, the sections do not share the pane's height, so none scrolls inside itself and the pane scrolls them all.
  A stage section is a `StageSection.vue`, a card between hairlines like the CPU's panels.
  A sidebar's choice among a few is a `ChoiceGroup.vue` (in equal columns where one row cannot hold them), and its width a `WidthPicker.vue`, the same group for 8, 16 and 32.
  Every page has a header of its own, so `useTopicTabFocus.ts` hands the focus from the topic that was pressed to the same button of the next page's activity bar; a topic's page puts the focus on its stage only when nothing else holds it.
  Below a desktop `TopicStrip.vue` keeps the operation in sight, and on a phone holds the way back to the CPU, whose header has no topic tabs there; `useTopicView.ts` holds a phone's view (Controls, or the stage), and `useTopicViewCommands.ts` builds the palette commands of the shell.
  `useLogicLens.ts` holds the circuit on show (the CPU's, or the learner's own after an edit: a "what if" never touches the CPU); its panels are `LogicOperation` and `LogicGatePanel` (the sidebar: which circuit, and the gate picked), `LogicAdder`, `LogicBitwise`, `LogicShifter` (rows of `LogicRow`), `LogicSlice` (one bit gate by gate, drawn from `logicSlice.ts`) and `LogicFlags`, with their text and layout in `logicView.ts`, `logicFlags.ts`, `logicShift.ts` and `logicLayout.ts`, and their styles in `styles/logic.css`.
  The adder can be slowed down: `useLogicTime.ts` holds the gate delay on show and plays through them (a flipped bit starts from the wires as they were, anything else from rest), `logicTime.ts` is its text and which bits will still change, `LogicTimeSwitch.vue` and `LogicTimeSteps.vue` are its controls in the topic's bar, and `LogicTimeNote.vue` says above the adder's rows what the wires are doing; the panels read the lens's `shown`, the circuit at that moment, and colour a write only once it is at rest.
  A gate can be broken: `useLogicFaults.ts` holds the gates the learner stuck at 0 or 1, kept while the circuit is the same one (so a fault follows the CPU from one instruction to the next) and never part of a CPU's circuit; the lens solves the request twice, `healthy` and broken, and `logicFault.ts` sets the two side by side (the wrong bits, the summary, what a stuck gate would put out).
  `LogicFault.vue` is the switch for the gate picked, in the sidebar's `LogicGatePanel` (on a phone, where the sidebar is another view, under the gate's truth table: the `gate` slot of `LogicDiagram.vue`), and `LogicFaultNote.vue` the line above the circuit.
  The gate picked can also be shown as NAND gates: `logicNand.ts` lays out the four kinds the circuits are drawn with (`nandDiagram`), really runs the gate's NAND gates on what its inputs carry (`nandSignals`), and counts what the whole circuit costs built that way (`nandCost`); `LogicNandSwitch.vue` turns it on, and `LogicNand.vue` draws it in the `under` slot of `LogicDiagram.vue`, through `LogicDrawing.vue`, the SVG both drawings share.
  What is picked (the bit, the shifter's row, the gate) is `useLogicChoice.ts`'s.
  The number page is built the same way: `useNumberLens.ts` holds the operation on show, and its panels are `NumbersOperation` (the sidebar: what to show, the width, `NumbersChoices`, what a lens sets once (a shift's direction and fill, an extension's fill and start, a decimal sum's operation; `numbersChoices.ts`), and `NumbersExtra`, the one more coming in), `NumbersArithmetic` (two `NumbersWheel`s, one per scale, drawn from `numbersWheel.ts`), `NumbersShift` (its controls and each scale's line in `numbersShift.ts`), `NumbersExtension`, `NumbersBcd`, `NumbersValue` (its rows in `numbersValue.ts`, and under them `NumbersBytes`, the value's bytes in memory in the order the lens holds, with its cells and text in `numbersBytes.ts`; its test stores a word on each core and compares the bytes) and `NumbersFlags`, with their text in `numbersView.ts` and `numbersFlags.ts` and their styles in `styles/numbers.css`; its rows of bits are the logic page's `LogicRow`.
  Under a difference with nothing more taken off, `NumbersCompare.vue` lists the conditional jumps of the CPU on show and whether each is taken: `numbersConditions.ts` holds a `ConditionSet` per architecture (the question each jump stands for, and how the CPU decides it from the flags of A − B; the 8086's through `JUMP_RULES`), and `numbersCompare.ts` says whether a view is a comparison (`isComparison`: never after an instruction that is not `comparable`) and sets each jump against the numbers (`compareRows`, the summary of the two scales, and the warning for a jump that does not always answer its question, as the 6502's BMI and BPL).
  The storage page is the third: `useStorageLens.ts` holds the register on show, which of the registers the instruction wrote it is, and the moment of the clock cycle (it opens before the clock rises), and `useStorageCell.ts` the memory cell drawn gate by gate, with the inputs and the held wires of a cell worked by hand.
  Its panels are `StorageRegister` (rows of `LogicRow` and the clock button) and `StorageCell`, with their text in `storageView.ts` and the drawings of the four cells in `storageDiagram.ts`; it has no style sheet of its own, and every circuit on it keeps its clock button beside its wires, since each has a clock of its own (its sidebar only picks which circuit each section shows).
  A cell worked by hand keeps a trace, its wires after each change (the last 16): `storageTiming.ts` turns it into the waves of a timing diagram and says what the last step did, and `StorageTiming.vue` draws them under the cell's inputs.
  Below the cell is the register file: `useStorageFile.ts` holds the file on show (the CPU's, or one of eight registers to work by hand; an address bit or the write line flipped is a "what if") and the moment of its own clock cycle, `storageFile.ts` its rows and text, and `StorageFile.vue` draws it; a register pressed there is put on show in the register above (`show` of `useStorageLens`).
  Last on the page are the flip-flops that feed one another, worked by hand: `useStorageSequence.ts` holds which circuit is on show (the shift register or the counter), its inputs, its wires and its trace, `storageSequence.ts` its rows of bits, its waves (through `chartOf` of `storageTiming.ts`, the drawing any trace shares) and what each tick did in words, and `StorageSequence.vue` draws it; `StorageTiming.vue` only draws the chart and the text it is handed.
  The control page is the fourth: `useControlLens.ts` holds the instruction word on show (the CPU's, or the learner's own after a bit is flipped or a word is typed), the decoders solved twice, `healthy` and with the lines the learner broke (`useControlFaults.ts`: the lines stuck at 0 or 1 and the one picked, kept from one instruction of the CPU to the next and never part of what a CPU ran), what the datapath does with the word (`outcome`, and `wholeOutcome` with every line working; both against the context of the CPU's instruction, so a changed word is followed as if it stood in its place) and the moment of PC's clock cycle.
  Its panels are `ControlWord` (the 32 bits), `ControlFields` (the fields they fall into: a section of the sidebar, and under the bits on a phone), `ControlPlane` (one decoder as a table: a row per AND gate, a column per input and per line, laid out by `controlPlane.ts`; used once for each decoder), `ControlWordEntry` (the word in hex, to type another, and what it reads as in assembly), `ControlLines` (every line as a button to pick it, those that are 1 with the way into the topic that shows what each switches, and the summary of what is broken; `ControlFault` is the switch that breaks the line picked, with their text in `controlFault.ts`), `ControlEffect` (what the datapath did, a row per part, each beside what it would be with every line working: `controlEffect.ts`) and `ControlPc` (the two sums, the lines that choose, and the clock button), with their text in `controlView.ts` and the table's styles in `styles/control.css`.
  `LogicDiagram.vue` draws any `SliceDiagram` with the truth table of the gate picked, for `LogicSlice` and `StorageCell` alike.
  `OperationLinks.vue` is the way in from a CPU's ALU panel: one `TopicLinkButton` per topic, dashed and saying why where the topic has nothing to show (`topicLinks.ts`); the way into the storage topic is the same button in the header of each registers panel, since an instruction that only moves a value never uses the ALU, and the way into the control topic is the same button in the `link` slot of `CycleStrip.vue` (and in the header of `Rv32Pipeline.vue`), shown where the strip has room for it.
  A CPU's history gives the snapshot an instruction left through `afterOf` (`useCpuHistory.ts`).
  The commands every topic shares (topics, preferences) come from `useAppCommands.ts`, and those of every topic that follows a CPU (the way back, the steps) from `momentCommands.ts`; `useLogicCommands.ts`, `useNumbersCommands.ts`, `useStorageCommands.ts` and `useControlCommands.ts` build each page's palette.
  Sound is three files: `soundPalette.ts` (every sound as data: short voices of filtered noise and sine or triangle tones), `soundEngine.ts` (plays them through Web Audio: `sound.play(name)`, with the master chain, the per-sound cooldown and the cap on voices; it makes its `AudioContext` only after a user gesture and is silent in a hidden tab) and `sounds.ts` (which sound an executed instruction makes on each architecture).
  `useSoundEffects.ts` plays what a CPU does (phases, steps, undo and redo, errors, program edits; a fast run is one pulse per line); the few sounds of the interface itself (a builder pick, a switch, the architecture) are played where they happen.
  `usePreferences.ts` turns the engine on and off with the Sound setting.
  The opening animation is plain HTML in `index.html`, drawn by `styles/splash.css`; an inline script there sets the stored theme and reduced motion before the first paint and skips the animation with reduced motion or after its first showing in a session (`?splash=1` plays it again).
  Once `main.ts` has mounted the app, `splash.ts` moves the chip into the header's mark (`data-splash-target` in `HeaderShell.vue`), landing a copy of that mark on it, or fades it where the header shows no mark; the first press or key ends it at once, and it takes that press so nothing behind it is clicked unseen.
  Theme tokens (including the app accents `--changed` and `--pointer`) live in `src/ui/styles.css`; colour is kept for those two signals (blue reads and where execution is, orange writes), and everything else is neutral.
  The rest of the styling is in `src/ui/styles/`, one sheet per area: `logic.css` (the logic topic), `numbers.css` (the number topic), `control.css` (the decoder tables of the control topic), `surfaces.css` (the shell: the two panes of the workbench at each width, with flat panels between hairlines; each pane scrolls on its own, and off a phone the sidebar's open panels share its height instead), `sidebar.css` (the sidebar's panels: collapsing, closing and resizing), `controls.css`, `panels.css`, `diagram.css` and `devices.css`.
